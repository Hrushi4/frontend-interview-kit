# Portfolio Site: Build Prompt and Ideas

Copy the prompt in Part 1 into Claude, v0, Lovable, Cursor or any other coding agent. Part 2 explains the ideas behind it, so you can add or remove features before you send it.

Things you must supply (the prompt tells the agent to leave clearly marked slots for them):

- A headshot: plain background, natural light, square, at least 800 × 800 px.
- Your résumé PDF, without your phone number if the site is public.
- Because of your NDA: no real screenshots. Recreate 2–3 key screens per project with sample data (for example in Figma), and confirm which résumé numbers you are allowed to publish.
- Your measurement details: how each number on your résumé was measured, and over what period.

# 2. What the site must do

- In 10 seconds, a recruiter knows: my name, my role, my years of experience, what
  I'm good at (real-time video, AI pipelines, frontend architecture, security), and
  how to get my résumé.
- In 2 minutes, a hiring manager finds one case study that shows how I think:
  problem, constraints, decisions, trade-offs, results.
- In an interview, I can screen-share a case study page and walk through it.

# 4. Visual design: restrained, editorial, professional

Palette (exactly these; no other colours):
- Light: background #FAFAF8, surface #FFFFFF, text #15171A, secondary text #5C626B,
  border #E5E5E0, accent #1F4FD1 (links, focus ring and one primary button only).
- Dark: background #0F1114, surface #16191D, text #E8EAED, secondary #9AA0A8,
  border #262A30, accent #7C9BFF.
- Follow the system theme, with a small manual toggle.
- No gradients, glows, glassmorphism or coloured shadows. Use a hairline border or a
  very soft neutral shadow only.

Typography:
- One sans family for everything (Geist, Inter or IBM Plex Sans), plus its mono
  only for dates and small numeric labels.
- At most 5 sizes (about 14, 16, 20, 28 and 44 px). Headings at weight 600, never 800.
- Body line length 60 to 75 characters. Generous line height (1.6).
- Sentence case everywhere. No all-caps letter-spaced labels above every heading.

Layout:
- A 12-column grid with a max content width of about 1080 px and a lot of white space.
- Left-aligned text. Consistent vertical rhythm (multiples of 8 px).
- Mobile first. Must look right at 360 px wide with no horizontal scroll.

# 5. Three.js: one purposeful use, not decoration

Use Three.js in exactly ONE place, where it explains my work:
- An "architecture explorer" on the medical video platform case study: a calm, isometric 3D
  diagram of the system: Browser/PWA → LiveKit SFU (AWS) → TURN relay → Redis →
  FastAPI → Postgres.
- Monochrome boxes with thin edges; the accent colour only on the selected part.
- Click or tab to a component to show a side panel: what it does, why it was chosen,
  and the trade-off.
- A "Simulate network drop" control animates media rerouting through the TURN relay,
  then recovering. Keep it subtle and slow, with no flashing.
- Include an accessible 2D fallback (an SVG diagram plus the same text) for
  reduced-motion users, no WebGL, and screen readers. Lazy-load Three.js only
  when this section scrolls into view.

Optional, only if it stays subtle: a small monochrome dot-network in the home intro
(under 300 points, slow, not behind any text, disabled on mobile and with reduced
motion). If in doubt, leave it out.

# 6. Banned (these make a site look AI-generated)

Never use:
- Emoji in the UI, a waving hand, "Hi, I'm …", or "Welcome to my portfolio".
- Gradient text, rainbow or conic rings, neon, glow, glass panels, particle or blob
  backgrounds behind text, or more than one accent colour.
- Highlighter or marker effects, sparkles, typing animations, cursor followers, fake
  terminals, fake dashboards or HUDs.
- Taglines like "I craft delightful/seamless/effortless experiences", or the words
  "passionate", "innovative", "cutting-edge", "pixel-perfect", "rockstar", "ninja".
- Exclamation marks, or "Let's build something amazing together".
- Grids of giant stat cards. Show numbers inside sentences or in one quiet results
  list per case study.
- Tech-logo walls, or skill chips on every card.
- Stock illustrations, AI-generated images, made-up testimonials, made-up client
  logos, or skill percentage bars.
- Scroll-jacking, or animations that delay reading content.

# 7. Writing voice

- Example intro: "I'm a senior software engineer in Mumbai. I build real-time video
  and AI products in React and Node.js, most recently for healthcare and call centres."
- Every number has a source line in its case study ("Measured from … over …").

- WCAG 2.2 AA: contrast ≥ 4.5:1, visible focus rings, a skip link, semantic

- Email shown as text with a copy button (don't rely on mailto alone).

  Ready for a custom domain later.
- README with: how to run, how to add a case study, and where to replace images.

## Part 2: Ideas, and why each one helps

Ordered by value for interviews. Build the first five; add the others when you have time.

### Must have

1. **Case studies with decisions, not features.**
   - Interviewers ask "why did you choose X?" A page that already lays out the options, the choice and the trade-off shows senior thinking before they ask.
   - It also becomes your script when you screen-share in an interview.

2. **A source line under every number.**
   - Your résumé is full of numbers (99%, 85%, 60%, 5x).
   - A one-line "how measured" note under each one builds trust and stops the first follow-up question.

3. **One real diagram per project.**
   - For example, the video call flow, the AI scoring pipeline, or the authorisation model, drawn with standard components only (NDA).
   - Real diagrams separate engineers from template sites. Draw them in Excalidraw or as SVG, not as stock art.

4. **Recreated interface images instead of screenshots (NDA).**
   - Real screenshots would break your NDA, so recreate the key patterns (pre-join check, reconnecting state, scorecard) with sample data and label them as recreations.
   - Being open about the NDA reads as professional. Interviewers respect "I can explain the approach but not share the code".

5. **A plain intro with the résumé one click away.**
   - Recruiters scan; the PDF and email must be visible without scrolling.
   - A real headshot makes the page feel human. Put it next to your name, small, with no effects.

### Strong additions

6. **A Three.js architecture explorer** (see the prompt, section 5).
   - It's the one place 3D earns its place: it helps someone understand LiveKit on AWS with a TURN relay.
   - A "network drop" walkthrough turns your reconnect story into something they can watch.

7. **Engineering notes: three short posts (500–800 words each).**
   - "Keeping the LiveKit Room out of React state"
   - "Testing authorisation for every role on every endpoint"
   - "Making LLM output safe to store"
   - Each one shows depth, ranks on Google for your name, and doubles as interview preparation.

8. **A small open-source piece (your best answer to the NDA).**
   - For example, an accessible video-call control bar, or a React hook for reconnection state.
   - Your company code is private, so a public repo is proof of how you write code.

9. **An accessibility note.**
   - One short section on how the site meets WCAG: keyboard use, contrast and reduced motion.
   - It's rare in portfolios, and directly backs up your accessibility work on the healthcare platform.

### Nice to have

10. **A "Now" line** in the About section: what you're learning or building this month. It keeps the site feeling current without a blog schedule.
11. **A printable résumé page** (/resume) that matches the site's typography, so the PDF and the site feel like one brand.
12. **Analytics without cookies** (Plausible or Umami). After applying, you can see whether a company actually opened your case study.

### Avoid (ideas that look impressive but hurt)

- Particle or 3D backgrounds behind the whole page.
- "Interview mode", fake terminals or anything game-like on the public site. Keep presenter notes private.
- Many accent colours, bright gradients, emoji, or playful copy.
- Listing every tool you've touched. Show the ones in your case studies.
